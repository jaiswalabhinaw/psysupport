Understanding your score

What your score means

Your score is a snapshot — a number that shows how much of each feeling you're experiencing right now. It doesn't label you or define you. These bands help you understand where you fall and what might help. You can read this section without taking any questionnaire if you just want to understand what the scores mean.

Mood Check score, 0–27
ScoreWhat it might meanWhat could help
0–4You're doing okay Your mood is in a good place right now. What matters is protecting the things that are keeping you steady.
5–9A bit of struggle You're noticing some low mood. This is when understanding what triggers it — and what helps — can prevent it from settling in.
10–14Noticeably affecting you Low mood is getting in the way. Talking to someone — a friend, family member, or a counsellor — becomes really valuable here.
15–19Significantly affecting you This is heavy to carry alone. Speaking to a counsellor or doctor isn't an overreaction — it's the right step.
20–27Urgent support needed You shouldn't have to manage this by yourself. Reach out to a counsellor, doctor or someone you trust today.

One question asks about thoughts of harming yourself. If you answer anything other than “not at all,” please reach out to someone you trust or a counsellor. You don't have to manage that alone.

Anxiety Check score, 0–21
ScoreWhat it might meanWhat could help
0–4You're managing well Worry isn't taking up much space. Keep doing what's working to stay calm.
5–9Noticing some worry Worry is showing up, but you're still in control. Now is the time to notice what sets it off and what calms you down.
10–14Worry is getting in the way Anxiety is affecting your sleep, focus or how you feel about things. Talking to someone, or learning some strategies, can help a lot.
15–21Anxiety is heavy Worry is wearing you down. Speaking to a counsellor or doctor makes real sense at this point.
Stress Check score, 0–18
ScoreWhat it might meanWhat could help
0–4You have space to breathe Pressure is manageable and you're getting recovery time. Keep protecting these recovery moments.
5–8Running warm You're feeling the pressure building. This is when it helps to notice what you might be dropping and what actually matters most.
9–12Stressed and depleted You're running on fumes. This needs to change — whether that's less load, more support, or finally taking time to recover.
13–18At the edge You're burning out. Pushing harder won't help. You need actual change — rest, support, talking to someone, or a different approach.

The stress check is a PsySupport reflection rather than a validated instrument, so its bands are a guide only. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 bands are the published ones.

Important to know

What these scores do and don't mean

It's a snapshot, not a diagnosis

These questionnaires capture how you're feeling right now — not what's causing it, and not a label that defines you. They're meant to help you understand yourself, not to put you in a box.

A low score doesn't mean you're fine

If you're struggling, it matters. You don't need to hit a high score to deserve support or to talk to someone. How you feel is reason enough.

Your score is just the beginning

Sleep, hormones, life events, medication, grief — so many things can shift your score. Understanding what's actually going on takes a real conversation with someone who knows you.

For reference

Other check-ins professionals might use

You may come across these names if you talk to a counsellor or see something online. We've listed them here so they feel less mysterious. Some are simple questionnaires like the ones above; others are longer tools that professionals use to understand the full picture. PsySupport doesn't administer all of these — just the mood and anxiety checks above.

Mood and depression

PHQ-9 — nine questions, the most widely used depression screen in general practice. Available above.

BDI-II (Beck Depression Inventory) — 21 items, more detailed, usually given by a clinician.

DASS-21 — 21 items covering depression, anxiety and stress in one questionnaire.

EPDS — used specifically around pregnancy and after childbirth.

Anxiety and panic

GAD-7 — seven questions for generalised anxiety. Available above.

Beck Anxiety Inventory — 21 items, weighted towards the physical side of anxiety.

SPIN — for social anxiety specifically.

PDSS — measures how severe and how frequent panic attacks are.

Stress, trauma and sleep

PSS (Perceived Stress Scale) — how unpredictable and overwhelming life has felt recently.

PCL-5 — a 20-item checklist for post-traumatic stress symptoms.

PSQI — a structured look at sleep quality over the last month.

MBI (Maslach Burnout Inventory) — exhaustion, cynicism and reduced effectiveness at work.

Personality, self-esteem and relationships

Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale — ten quick statements about how you regard yourself.

Big Five / NEO — personality across five traits, often used for self-understanding rather than diagnosis.

MMPI-2 — 567 items, a formal clinical instrument administered and interpreted by a psychologist.

Dyadic Adjustment Scale — used in couples work to look at relationship satisfaction.

Cognition and substance use

MMSE and MoCA — short cognitive screens, usually used with older adults.

WAIS — a full intelligence assessment, administered one-to-one by a psychologist.

AUDIT and CAGE — brief screens for alcohol use.
