Score guide

What each score actually means

You do not have to take a test to read these. Each questionnaire adds up to a single number, and that number falls into one of a few bands. The bands below are the standard ones clinicians use — they describe how much you are reporting, not what is causing it.

PHQ-9 depression score, 0–27
ScoreBandWhat it usually means, and what tends to help
0–4Minimal The range most people fall into on an ordinary fortnight. If something still feels hard, that counts — no threshold is required to talk to someone.
5–9Mild A few symptoms showing up regularly. Patterns are usually still loose here and shift comparatively easily. Worth watching if it has run more than a month.
10–14Moderate Enough symptoms, often enough, to be affecting concentration, energy and self-image. The point at which talking to a counsellor or doctor is a reasonable step rather than an overreaction.
15–19Moderately severe Most symptoms present on most days. Tiring to live with, and not something willpower is meant to fix. Counselling, plus a doctor’s view on anything physical contributing, are both worth arranging.
20–27Severe Nearly everything present nearly every day. A strong signal not to carry this alone. Speaking to a doctor or mental health professional soon is the right next step.

Question 9 of the PHQ-9 asks about thoughts of self-harm. Any answer above “not at all” is treated as important on its own, whatever the total comes to.

GAD-7 anxiety score, 0–21
ScoreBandWhat it usually means, and what tends to help
0–4Minimal Anxiety symptoms are not showing up often. Whatever is keeping you steady is worth protecting.
5–9Mild Present, but not dominating the fortnight. A good point to learn what sets it off and what settles it, before the habits around it harden.
10–14Moderate Taking up real space — sleep, concentration and patience usually go first. Structured support genuinely helps here, and waiting tends to make it harder.
15–21Severe Most symptoms on most days. That level of alertness wears down the body as well as the mind. Worth arranging counselling, and a doctor’s view on anything physical feeding it.
Stress & burnout check, 0–18
ScoreBandWhat it usually means, and what tends to help
0–4Low Pressure is there but you are absorbing it. The recovery you are getting is doing its job.
5–8Moderate Running warm. This is the stage where people quietly drop the things that were keeping them steady.
9–12High The pattern that becomes burnout if it runs long enough — depleted, short-tempered, unable to switch off, doing it alone. Responds well to support, better early.
13–18Very high Close to burnout: energy gone, meaning gone, nobody sharing the load. Not a personal failing, and it does not resolve by pushing harder.

The stress check is a PsySupport reflection rather than a validated instrument, so its bands are a guide only. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 bands are the published ones.

What a score can and cannot tell you

It is a screen, not a verdict

These questionnaires measure how much distress you are reporting over the last two weeks. They cannot tell you what is causing it, and they cannot diagnose anything on their own.

A low score is not a dismissal

Plenty of people score in the mild range and still find things genuinely hard. If something is affecting your life, that is reason enough to talk about it — no threshold required.

Context changes the meaning

Poor sleep, thyroid problems, grief, exam pressure and a new medication can all move a score. That is exactly the kind of thing a conversation sorts out and a questionnaire cannot.

Good to know

Other assessments psychologists use

You may hear these names in a clinic or online. Most are screening questionnaires like the two above; a few are long, formally administered instruments that only a trained psychologist can score and interpret. They are listed here so the language is less intimidating — PsySupport does not administer the clinical instruments in this list.

Mood and depression

PHQ-9 — nine questions, the most widely used depression screen in general practice. Available above.

BDI-II (Beck Depression Inventory) — 21 items, more detailed, usually given by a clinician.

DASS-21 — 21 items covering depression, anxiety and stress in one questionnaire.

EPDS — used specifically around pregnancy and after childbirth.

Anxiety and panic

GAD-7 — seven questions for generalised anxiety. Available above.

Beck Anxiety Inventory — 21 items, weighted towards the physical side of anxiety.

SPIN — for social anxiety specifically.

PDSS — measures how severe and how frequent panic attacks are.

Stress, trauma and sleep

PSS (Perceived Stress Scale) — how unpredictable and overwhelming life has felt recently.

PCL-5 — a 20-item checklist for post-traumatic stress symptoms.

PSQI — a structured look at sleep quality over the last month.

MBI (Maslach Burnout Inventory) — exhaustion, cynicism and reduced effectiveness at work.

Personality, self-esteem and relationships

Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale — ten quick statements about how you regard yourself.

Big Five / NEO — personality across five traits, often used for self-understanding rather than diagnosis.

MMPI-2 — 567 items, a formal clinical instrument administered and interpreted by a psychologist.

Dyadic Adjustment Scale — used in couples work to look at relationship satisfaction.

Cognition and substance use

MMSE and MoCA — short cognitive screens, usually used with older adults.

WAIS — a full intelligence assessment, administered one-to-one by a psychologist.

AUDIT and CAGE — brief screens for alcohol use.
