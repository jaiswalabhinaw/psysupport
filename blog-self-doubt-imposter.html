Self-Doubt and Feeling Like a Fraud

By Abhinaw • 14 July 2026 • 5 min read

You did the work. It went well. And somewhere underneath is the quiet conviction that you got away with something, and that eventually someone will notice.

This is common, and it is notably common among people who are competent. That is the strange part.

Why Achievement Does Not Fix It

The obvious solution is more evidence. Do well again, and surely the feeling settles.

It does not, because the belief has a built-in defence. Success gets explained away: it was luck, it was easy, the standard was low, they were being kind, anyone could have done it. Failure, meanwhile, is taken as confirmation. With those rules, no amount of evidence can ever accumulate.

This is why reassurance from others provides relief for about an hour. It is being poured into a container with a hole in it.

Common Shapes It Takes

  • Over-preparation. Working far beyond what a task requires, then crediting the outcome to the effort rather than to ability.
  • Avoiding stretch. Staying safely inside proven competence so exposure never happens.
  • Deflecting credit. Redirecting praise so fast that it never lands.
  • Comparing your inside to their outside. Measuring your private uncertainty against other people's public composure.
  • Perfectionism. Where anything short of flawless registers as failure, and flawless registers as the minimum.

What Actually Shifts It

Notice the discounting as it happens. The useful moment is not the doubt itself, it is the instant you explain away something that went well. That is the mechanism, and it can be caught.

Keep a plain record. Write down what you did and what resulted, factually, without interpretation. Not to feel better, but to have something the discounting habit cannot easily rewrite later.

Separate feeling from fact. Feeling unqualified and being unqualified are different claims. The first is a sensation. The second requires evidence.

Say it out loud to one person. These feelings survive on secrecy. Most people are quietly startled to find how widespread it is.

Aim for adequate. Deliberately doing something to a sufficient rather than perfect standard, and observing that the consequences do not arrive, is more persuasive than any amount of reasoning.

When It Is Worth Addressing Properly

Occasional self-doubt is ordinary and arguably useful. It is worth working on when it is stopping you applying, speaking, or taking opportunities you are capable of, when it is driving overwork you cannot sustain, or when it sits alongside persistent low mood. The pattern is usually older than the job it currently attaches itself to, which is why talking it through tends to be more effective than trying to reason with it alone.
