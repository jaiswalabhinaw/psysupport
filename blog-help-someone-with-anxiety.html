How to Help Someone With Anxiety

By Abhinaw • 28 May 2026 • 5 min read

Watching someone you love struggle with anxiety is genuinely difficult. You want to fix it. You cannot. That gap is uncomfortable, and it often leads well-meaning people to say things that land badly.

What Tends Not to Help

  • "Just relax" or "calm down." If that were available to them, they would have used it.
  • "There is nothing to worry about." Anxiety is rarely resolved by being told it is unreasonable. The person usually already knows.
  • "Others have it worse." This adds guilt to distress.
  • Immediately problem-solving. Sometimes useful, often premature. Solutions offered before someone feels heard tend to land as dismissal.
  • Taking avoidance personally. Cancelled plans are usually about capacity, not about you.

What Actually Helps

Listen without rushing to fix. "That sounds really hard, tell me more" does more work than any advice. Being genuinely heard reduces distress on its own.

Validate the feeling, not the worst-case story. You do not have to agree that the catastrophe will happen to acknowledge that the fear is real and exhausting. "I can see how much this is weighing on you" does both.

Ask what would help. People often know. Some want company, some want distraction, some want space. Guessing tends to miss.

Be steady and predictable. Anxiety thrives on uncertainty. Being someone whose responses are consistent is quietly stabilising.

Offer specific, practical help. "I can drive you" is easier to accept than "let me know if you need anything."

Notice the effort. Things that look small from outside, a phone call made, a room entered, can have taken real courage.

The Balance With Avoidance

This part is delicate. Anxiety shrinks when someone gradually faces what they fear, and grows when it is consistently avoided. So constantly stepping in to remove every difficulty can unintentionally reinforce the problem.

At the same time, pushing someone before they are ready damages trust and usually backfires.

The workable position is somewhere between: support them in taking steps at their own pace rather than either taking the steps for them or forcing them. And be honest with yourself about which accommodations are kindness and which have quietly become the two of you organising life around the anxiety.

Look After Yourself Too

Supporting someone with sustained anxiety is tiring, and this is under-acknowledged. You are allowed limits. You are allowed your own life, your own rest, and your own support. Being depleted does not make you more helpful.

You are also not their therapist, and trying to be usually strains the relationship you actually have.

Encouraging Professional Support

Raise it gently and without ultimatum. "I wonder whether talking to someone might take some of this weight off you" works better than "you need help." Offer to help with the practical side, finding options or sitting with them while they book.

If someone mentions thoughts of self-harm or suicide, take it seriously, stay with them, and get professional support involved. The helpline and emergency numbers are at the bottom of this page.
