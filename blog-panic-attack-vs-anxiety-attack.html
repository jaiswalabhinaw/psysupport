Panic Attack vs Anxiety: What Is the Difference?

By Abhinaw • 12 May 2026 • 5 min read

People use "panic attack" and "anxiety attack" as if they mean the same thing. They describe different experiences, and knowing which one you are having changes what helps.

A note on terms: "panic attack" is a recognised clinical description. "Anxiety attack" is an everyday phrase rather than a formal one, generally used to describe a period of intense anxiety. The distinction below is about the experience, not about diagnosing yourself.

How They Differ

Onset. Panic tends to arrive abruptly, sometimes with no identifiable trigger, occasionally waking people from sleep. Intense anxiety usually builds, and can often be traced to something specific.

Intensity. Panic peaks steeply, generally within about ten minutes. Anxiety can sit at a moderate level for hours or days.

Physical experience. Panic is overwhelmingly physical: pounding heart, breathlessness, shaking, sweating, chest tightness, numbness, a sense of unreality. Many people genuinely believe they are having a heart attack or dying. Anxiety is physical too, but usually less extreme.

Duration. Panic burns intensely and passes relatively quickly, though it often leaves you drained for hours. Anxiety is more of a sustained state.

What to Do During a Panic Attack

Remember that it peaks and falls. A panic attack cannot sustain itself indefinitely. Your body physically cannot hold that level of activation. This will pass, usually within minutes.

Do not fight it. Struggling against panic adds fear on top of fear. Counterintuitively, allowing it to be there, saying to yourself that this is unpleasant and not dangerous, tends to shorten it.

Slow the exhale. Do not try to take deep breaths in, which often makes over-breathing worse. Focus on breathing out slowly and fully.

Ground through your senses. Name five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell, one you can taste. This gives attention a concrete task.

Stay where you are if you safely can. Leaving immediately teaches your brain that the place was the danger, which makes the next episode more likely there.

What Helps With Ongoing Anxiety

Sustained anxiety responds less to in-the-moment techniques and more to what happens between episodes: sleep, physical activity, identifying what is actually generating the pressure, and having somewhere to think out loud. Grounding manages a spike. It does not resolve a pattern.

An Important Note

Because panic symptoms overlap with cardiac and respiratory conditions, do not self-diagnose a first episode. Chest pain and breathing difficulty deserve medical assessment. Once physical causes have been ruled out, the pattern becomes much easier to work with.

If panic attacks are recurring, or if you have begun avoiding places and situations because you are afraid of having one, that avoidance tends to expand over time. Support is worth seeking sooner rather than later.
